# Visual and multimedia principles: original synthesis

Based on the two user-provided visual-design excerpts (which attribute contrast, repetition, alignment, and proximity to Robin Williams and multimedia guidance to Richard Mayer). Treat practitioner preferences and tool endorsements separately from learning principles. No claim is made to have reviewed Mayer's full book or validated the excerpts' product recommendations.

| Principle | Operational rule | Check |
|---|---|---|
| Contrast | Different functions should be distinguishable through size, weight, position, and color | Task and heading stand apart from support text; color is not the sole status cue |
| Repetition | Reuse approved styles and interaction conventions | Buttons, focus states, feedback, and navigation behave consistently across lessons |
| Alignment | Use shared edges and an intentional grid | Related text, media, and controls align; responsive layouts preserve meaning |
| Proximity | Group related elements and separate unrelated groups | Caption near its image; response options clearly grouped with their prompt |
| White space | Give text and actions sufficient room without hiding content in oversized emptiness | No cramped labels or needless scrolling caused by decorative space |
| Balance | Distribute visual weight intentionally, not necessarily symmetrically | Illustration does not overpower the task or shrink critical copy |
| Hierarchy | Make attention follow the instructional purpose | Squint/blur inspection identifies the intended focus, not a decorative element |
| Coherence | Remove irrelevant words, graphics, sounds, and movement | Each visual/media element has a learning, orientation, or functional purpose |
| Signaling | Cue essential information at the moment it is needed | Highlights and labels direct attention without revealing an assessment answer |
| Spatial contiguity | Place explanatory labels next to corresponding visual elements | Learners need not search across the page to connect words and graphics |
| Temporal coordination | Align explanation and relevant visual change | Demonstration narration and state changes refer to the same step |
| Redundancy | Avoid unnecessary competition among lengthy on-screen prose, narration, and graphics | Do not make a universal ban on repeated words. Preserve captions/transcripts, key labels, technical terms, and learner-controlled text when needed for access or task demands |
| Segmenting | Break complex demonstrations into meaningful learner-controlled steps | Pause/replay/step controls support comprehension; no arbitrary time pressure |

A single font family with distinct weights and sizes can provide good contrast; two font families are not mandatory. Multimedia choices depend on task, learner knowledge, accessibility, and delivery context. Do not equate motion with interactivity or clicks with cognitive engagement.

Accessibility targets include text contrast of 4.5:1 for normal text and 3:1 for qualifying large text, visible focus, keyboard operation, captions/transcripts as appropriate, and meaningful reading order. Validate actual output; these are not a complete conformance checklist. Reference: [W3C contrast minimum](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/contrast-minimum.html).

Useful animation examples: show the data path that produces an error; highlight the field affected by a learner action; reveal an operational consequence after a decision. Prefer static or stepwise equivalents when motion is unnecessary. Audio is optional and must not be essential without an accessible equivalent.
