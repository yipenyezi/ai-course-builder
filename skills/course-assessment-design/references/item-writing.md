# Item-writing guidance and source decisions

Original synthesis of the user-provided item-writing excerpts and *Is This a Trick Question? A Short Guide to Writing Effective Test Questions*, Ben Clay, Kansas Curriculum Center (2001), supplied as Handout-Module6.pdf. Consulted relevant sections on alignment, test planning, MCQs, completion items, essays, and rubrics (printed pages 5, 8–10, 15–16, 36–37, 41–43, 48–49). The handout is classroom/paper-test guidance, not a modern digital accessibility standard or proof of workplace measurement quality. Full source files are not reproduced in this repository.

The supplied excerpts include The Learning Rooms-style seven-step guidance, a CME single-best-answer guide, and a 16-guideline list supplied directly in chat. Exact provenance for unattributed excerpts is not independently verified. Clinical examples and dates are not adopted as factual training content. This skill uses their design principles with the qualifications below.

## Format selection

| Format | Suitable evidence | Requirements / limits |
|---|---|---|
| Single-best-answer MCQ | Interpret cues, select a next action, distinguish alternatives | Focused problem and one defensible best answer; may assess reasoning without demonstrating performance |
| Multiple response | Identify a justified set of applicable actions | Explicit select-all or select-N instruction; approve scoring and partial credit. Harder guessing is not proof of better validity. |
| True/false | A narrow, genuinely binary claim when useful | Avoid context-dependent generalizations; acknowledge guessing and limited evidence. Do not use only for visual variety. |
| Matching | Relate homogeneous concepts or categories | State whether responses may repeat; accessible alternative to drag-only interaction; prevent easy elimination clues |
| Completion/short response | Retrieve or explain a defined concept | Clear expected scope, accepted variants, numeric units/precision if applicable; prefer blank at the end if using a sentence |
| Extended response/performance | Explain reasoning, create, operate, or demonstrate | Analytic criteria and anchors, sufficient opportunity, consistent review; avoid unrelated writing-quality penalties |
| Open AI role-play | Conduct a conversation through multiple decisions | Free input and context-sensitive rubric, not a hidden script; final personalized debrief only |

## Stem and scenario

- Ask a specific question or give an explicit task. Remove irrelevant words, not information needed to distinguish valid actions. A short prompt is not better if it omits a decisive fact.
- Use task-appropriate, plain language and necessary technical terms. Maintain taught terminology where changing it would change the construct. New application is created through reasoning/context, not merely paraphrasing vocabulary.
- Prefer positive wording. When identifying an unsafe or prohibited action is genuinely the task, make that requirement explicit; avoid double negatives.
- Second person is appropriate for workplace decisions and role-play. Third person is an option, not a blanket rule. Names should be easy to follow and relevant; do not exclude culturally diverse names or introduce stereotypes.
- Include only useful graphics, with readable labels and equivalent access to decision cues. Decorative art must not introduce answer clues or inaccessible essential information.

## Options and keys

- A single-best-answer key must remain best under the stated conditions. If several actions could be justified, clarify context or use another format. Never force one preferred sales phrase to be the only valid answer.
- Keep options in the same category and grammatical form. Move repeated context into the stem. Avoid overlapping numerical ranges or alternatives where one contains another.
- Plausible distractors represent a recognizable misconception, skipped step, misread cue, or premature action. Do not invent an “SME-confirmed” mistake. Ask the SME or mark it proposed.
- Avoid all/none-of-the-above and A-and-B combinations by default. Direct multi-select with clear rules is different from combination-coded “K-type” options.
- Preserve absolutes when an approved rule truly is absolute; otherwise avoid artificial always/never cues. Do not weaken an actual safety rule to make a distractor less obvious.
- Use similar length and specificity, not identical word counts or guaranteed line counts across screens. Move explanations to feedback. Inspect whether the key is habitually longer, more polished, or repeats the stem's distinctive wording.

## Reconciling source recommendations

| Source suggestion | Project application |
|---|---|
| At least two items per outcome | Confirm count by objective aspect, evidence needs, stakes, and available time. No automatic quota. |
| At least four alternatives | Prioritize plausible options; three may be sufficient. Do not claim option count alone establishes reliability. |
| Always third person; avoid contractions/punctuation | Favor clarity and realistic English. These are not universal measurement requirements. |
| New language increases cognitive complexity | A new reasoning demand can; word substitution alone does not. |
| Equal answer-position proportions | Avoid predictable clues across the set, but no forced exact balance or visible cycle. |
| Repeat questions for retrieval | Useful for practice; novel comparable tasks are needed when interpreting independent application or transfer. |
| More items improve reliability | Sampling, quality, purpose, and fatigue matter; length is not a guarantee. |

## Scoring, feedback, and review

Approve scoring/partial-credit rules before use; separate practice feedback from final assessment release. Feedback should explain the decision and next step, not merely announce a key. Open-answer rubrics should accept equivalent evidence and distinguish insufficient evidence from incorrect behavior. Do not disclose answer keys or hidden evaluation details through learner-visible telemetry.

Use pilot evidence to investigate unclear items and nonfunctioning distractors. For small samples, report counts and qualitative limitations; do not overinterpret unstable percentages or discrimination estimates. Expert review plus a pilot improves confidence but does not by itself certify the assessment.
